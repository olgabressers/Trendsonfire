Immersive venue financial analysis · public data only

The same ticket is worth a different amount in every country — and nobody has priced that

A financial model of eleven immersive art venues across seven countries, built the same way as the Remastered financial review: consumer prices stripped of value-added tax, one cost architecture applied consistently, adjusted for national labour cost, then break-even and a ten percent sensitivity. Every input is public. Every output is labelled as a model.

Profit and loss — the terms used on this page

Shelf price
What the visitor pays. In most of Europe this legally includes value-added tax, so it is not revenue.
Value-added tax (VAT)
A consumption tax inside the consumer price. Divide the shelf price by one plus the rate to get revenue.
Net revenue
The shelf price after VAT is removed. This is what the business actually earns per ticket.
Fixed cost
Cost that does not move with visitor numbers — staff, rent, content, utilities.
Variable cost
Cost incurred per visitor — here, ticketing commission and payment fees.
Contribution per visitor
Net revenue per visitor minus variable cost per visitor. What each extra visitor contributes to fixed costs.
Break-even
Fixed costs divided by contribution per visitor. The number of visitors at which the result is zero.
Margin of safety
How far modelled volume sits above break-even, as a percentage. Negative means below it.
Operating leverage
In a high-fixed-cost business, a small change in volume produces a large change in result.
Corporate income tax
Tax on profit, charged after the result is struck. Different in every country in this set.

What the price is worth
Value-added tax — the shelf price is not revenue
  • The headline price comparison is misleading and every venue in this set is affected differently. A €24.50 ticket in Rotterdam carries €2.02 of VAT. A €16.00 ticket in Luxembourg carries €0.47.
  • Luxembourg keeps 97.1% of the shelf price. Rotterdam keeps 91.7%. Bucharest keeps 90.1%. Bulgaria, with no reduced rate found for museum admission, would keep 83.3%.
  • Montreal keeps 100% — because it does not include the tax at all. OASIS publishes prices excluding taxes and fees, so the shelf price and the net revenue are the same number and the visitor pays the tax on top.
  • On a net-revenue basis the gap between the two most expensive venues almost disappears. On shelf price Remastered leads OASIS by 12%. Net of tax it leads by 3%.

What the price is worth
Corporate income tax — the same profit, taxed three ways
  • The spread across this set is three-fold. Bulgaria 10%, Romania 16%, Luxembourg 23.87%, Netherlands 25.80%, Canada 26.50%, Mexico 30%, Germany 30.06%.
  • A group holding company changes where the profit lands, and that is worth more than most operating synergies. It is also the part of a cross-border group structure that no benchmark on revenue and cost will ever surface.
  • The Netherlands charges 19% on the first €200,000 of profit before the 25.80% rate applies — relevant at the profit levels this model produces.

The cost side
National labour cost — a 4.7× spread across the set
  • An hour of labour costs €56.80 in Luxembourg and €12.00 in Bulgaria. Staff is the largest single cost line in this kind of venue, so this difference dominates everything else on the cost side.
  • Remastered sits near the top of the range at €47.90 — the second most expensive labour market in this set, behind Luxembourg.
  • MINA operates five venues in the two cheapest labour markets in the European Union. Its cost base is structurally different, not just smaller.
  • This is why a benchmark that compares cost categories without normalising for country tells you almost nothing. A Romanian venue will always look efficient and a Dutch one always look expensive, whatever either management does.

04
The cost side
Cost architecture — one structure, applied consistently

Method, stated in full

The cost architecture is carried over unchanged from the Remastered financial review: eight lines, seven fixed and one variable, totalling €2.17m at 97,000 visitors in a Dutch cost setting.

Only the staff line is adjusted by country, scaled by that country's hourly labour cost relative to the Netherlands. Every other line is held at the Dutch level, because no verified country index exists for rent, content, utilities or general administration in this sector.

That choice is deliberately conservative and it understates the low-cost venues. Rent in Wuppertal or Bucharest is not the same as rent beneath the Erasmusbrug. Holding it flat means the model flatters the Dutch venue and penalises the others — so where a low-cost venue still models poorly, the real position is better than shown.

Revenue uses net-of-VAT price from section 1 and the modelled visitor volumes from the venue benchmark, which are derived from published opening hours at one identical throughput assumption of 46 visitors per operating hour.

05
The model
Modelled profit and loss
  • On ticket revenue alone, only the highest-priced venue in the set covers a Western-European cost base. The modelled result at Rotterdam prices and volumes is €10,000 before tax — effectively zero.
  • Every other modelled venue falls short on tickets alone, by €426,000 to €654,000 at these volumes and this cost structure.
  • That is the real finding, and it is not about management. A 60-to-90 minute immersive show, sold at €16 to €18, at roughly 90,000 to 110,000 visitors a year, does not carry a €2m Western-European cost base on tickets.
  • Which means the non-ticket lines are not upside — they are the business. Corporate hire, food and drink, membership and combination tickets are what close a gap of this size, and the benchmark page shows exactly which venues run them and which do not.
  • Luxembourg models worst despite trading the longest week, because it combines the highest labour cost in the set with the second-lowest price.

06
The model
Break-even and margin of safety
  • The modelled Rotterdam position sits 0.5% above break-even. Break-even is 96,430 visitors; the modelled volume is 96,876. Four hundred and forty-six visitors of headroom on the year.
  • That is not a margin. It is a rounding error. A high-fixed-cost venue operating half a percent above break-even has no absorption capacity for a bad quarter, a content delay or a projector failure.
  • Every other modelled venue needs 27% to 48% more visitors than the model gives it to break even on tickets alone.
  • Luxembourg needs 153,896 visitors — 37% above its modelled volume — because high labour cost and a low price compress contribution from both ends.

07
The model
Ten percent sensitivity
  • A ten percent swing in visitors moves the modelled Rotterdam result by €417,000 — from −€199,000 to +€218,000, on revenue of €2.18m.
  • That is operating leverage, and it cuts both ways with equal force. Roughly 96% of modelled costs are fixed, so every visitor above break-even is almost pure margin and every visitor below it is almost pure loss.
  • It is also why a venue operating 0.5% above break-even is a fragile business rather than a profitable one. Ten percent is an ordinary year-to-year movement in this category.

What the model says
Three conclusions that only appear once the tax, the labour cost and the fixed-cost base are put on the same page.
01
Price comparison without tax is meaningless

The venue with the highest shelf price does not have the highest net revenue by the margin it appears to. Strip the tax and a 12% lead becomes 3%. No benchmark that compares ticket prices across borders is telling the truth unless it does this first.

02
Tickets do not carry the cost base

At these prices and volumes, only the most expensive venue in the set reaches break-even on ticket revenue alone — and it reaches it by half a percent. Corporate hire, hospitality, membership and combination tickets are not upside in this category. They are the difference between a business and a hobby.

03
Country beats management

A 4.7× labour-cost spread and a 3× corporate-tax spread across this set mean a Romanian venue will always benchmark as efficient and a Dutch one as expensive, regardless of how either is run. Any comparison that skips this ranks countries and calls it performance.

Limits
What the model cannot see

Outside the model — stated rather than hidden

  • All non-ticket revenue. Corporate hire, food and drink, retail, membership, combination tickets, licensing and touring. None of it is public for any venue, and section 5 shows it is the difference between loss and profit.
  • Real visitor numbers. The model uses one throughput assumption for every venue because no venue publishes actual footfall.
  • Real cost levels. Only staff is country-adjusted. Rent, content, utilities and administration are held at Dutch levels for every venue.
  • The blended ticket price. Adult price is used throughout. Children, groups, schools, discount cards and reseller rates all pull the real average below it, for every venue equally.
  • Reseller commission. Online travel agents typically charge 20–30%, and the share of tickets sold through them is not public for any venue.
  • Seasonality. Annual figures are straight-line. Every venue in this category trades unevenly across the year.
  • Capital structure. Debt, interest, shareholder loans and the original build-out financing.
  • Lease terms and remaining duration. Whether a building is owned, leased, or occupied under a public-sector arrangement changes the cost base entirely.
  • Content ownership. Whether a show is owned, co-produced or licensed, and on what revenue-share terms.
  • Grants and public funding. At least one venue in the set is constituted as a gGmbH — a German non-profit form — which implies a different funding model the accounts would reveal and the website does not.
